*What Stands in a Storm* is an account of the April 2011 Southeast superstorm, an outbreak that saw 358 tornadoes rip across the Southeast in the largest and deadliest outbreak in recorded history, causing $11 billion in damage and killing 348 people. Cross does a solid job going from macro to micro, leaning into the emotional angle.

From the big picture, we don't really know what causes tornadoes. Yes, it's rotating air, but most rotating supercells don't spawn tornadoes. The National Weather Service has about an 80% false positive rate on their tornado predictions. That, combined with the narrow track of destruction, means that people often don't take tornado warnings as seriously as they should. Legendary Alabama weatherman James Spann is working on increasing public understanding of storms and getting agencies to understand that a prediction that makes people ignore a real storm is as damaging as missing a tornado.

Not that precision was needed heading into April 27, 2011. Everyone could tell this one was going to be bad. As the storm approached, Cross narrows in on her exemplar victims, three college students sheltering in a house on Beverly Heights. Danielle was a senior in social work and a bubbly light at her job at a local hotel, set to graduate in two weeks. Loryn was the daughter of a UA football champion and dreamed of going into sports reporting. Will grew up on a farm and played baseball at Stillman, the historically black college in Tuscaloosa. He was ten days from graduating, and planned to become a teacher and coach at his old high school. He went to the house to keep the girls company.

Cross draws out the communications and fear of the trio, and then the sudden radio silence as the storm hit their neighborhood. Their parents were anxious, then terrified, and then moving through all the stages of grief as it became apparent that the silence from their children wasn't just cell disruption, but something serious. The house the girls shared was surrounded by old trees, and those oaks and gums turned into battering rams in the force of the EF4, which tore through Tuscaloosa. The three were doing the right thing, sheltering in the central hallway. Their house was a deathtrap.

The back half of the book follows the search and recovery process. One of the strange qualities of tornadoes is their extremely localized damage. As soon as the storm passed, ordinary people went into the devastated zone with whatever they had and started pulling people out of rubble and clearing trees. Emergency services were overwhelmed, and several material stockpiles got smashed by the storm, but the human element worked at an unstinting pace. Meanwhile, Danielle, Loryn, and Will's parents went through the horrible process of identifying their children's bodies and making sense of the senseless. The deep Christianity that pervaded the region proved a pillar here. "They're in heaven now" was about the only solace available.

This book chooses emotion over the more intellectual approach of [*Isaac's Storm: A Man, a Time, and the Deadliest Hurricane in History*](/book/isaacs-storm-a-man-a-time-and-the-deadliest-hurricane-in-history-larson), and is lesser for it. There is enough examination of the science and social complexities of living with tornadoes to make this book serious non-fiction, but this is not a particularly technical book, aside from a general call for better weather forecasts.

It strikes me that many of the issues around tornados are architectural. Good warnings won't help if you're in a shack in the path of an EF4, but conversely hiding in a bunker every time the sky darkens is unrealistic. There should be usable fortified spaces at hand, but that costs money in a state that is both impoverished and profoundly opposed to government intervention. That's the systemic judgment Cross is unwilling to make. Not that disaster preparedness is an American trait. Every state has its own pile of hazards. As a Californian, I think Mike Davis is right in [The Case for Letting Malibu Burn](https://longreads.com/2018/12/04/the-case-for-letting-malibu-burn/). Choosing to build and rebuild mansions in a fire zone is a very similar error to not building basements in Tornado Alley.

Disaster books should have a lesson, and the lesson here is that children dying is awful. Using picture-perfect innocents as the primary subjects would be a cheap trick in lesser hands. Cross treats these individual tragedies with immense respect and compassion. I am not immune to sentimentality, and Cross is a skilled writer who crafts a compelling narrating, but I wish she had something more insightful to say.
