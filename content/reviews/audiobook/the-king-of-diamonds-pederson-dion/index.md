Pederson was a newly minted reporter working in Dallas in the early 1970s when she first heard about a daring jewel thief. The thief, whom the newspapers had dubbed the King of Diamonds, had robbed scores of Dallas mansions over more than a decade. He seemed fearless, breaking into occupied homes and hiding in closets until their owners went to sleep, stealing only the best jewels while leaving many other valuable pieces behind, and leaving almost no traces, aside from the occasional waffle-sole shoe print. The police were powerless to catch him.

Shortly after Pederson started her career, the thefts stopped, and she moved on to other beats. Forty-five years later, retired from the papers and a grandmother with wide-ranging tastes in mystery fiction, she decided to go back and see if she could crack the case. More than a story about crime, this is a story about Dallas in the 1960s, about the glamour of oil money and how money shaded into organized crime and political corruption.

Pederson takes her time going through the victims, a who's who of the wealthiest people in Dallas, like oil tycoons Clint Murchison and H.L. Hunt. Upper-echelon Dallas society was organized around big parties and the debutante circle, but beneath the glittering facade was deep weirdness and rot. Murchison's dealings blended the legal, the rule-bending, and the outright criminal. *Everyone* in Dallas gambled, playing cards deep into the night at underground casinos like Top O'Hill Terrace, which included tunnels so that guests could escape raids from the Texas Rangers. Modern art connoisseurs rubbed shoulders with notorious strippers like Candy Barr, with mafia made men lurking in the background.

Even with many digressions, the diamond thefts are what the book returns to. They were distinctly odd, often targeted when the victims had just returned from a party and were unlikely to lock up the gems they wore. The thief appeared to know the layouts of the houses and moved precisely to dressing rooms. Staff and children heard nothing, and even dogs did not bark. Most bizarrely, he would pick through collections inside the houses when most thieves toss everything into a sack and sort it out at home. He deliberately ruined sets by taking only one of a pair of earrings, as if he were sending a message. Suspicion spread like wildfire. Domestic servants were polygraphed. Famous interior designers, landscape architects, and hairdressers fell under the cloud. The elite even began to suspect each other: "Are you the King of Diamonds?" was a joke on the party circuit for years.

The King of Diamonds was never caught. Decades after the crimes, Pederson thinks she's identified the thief in the son of a wealthy local family. This would neatly explain the thief's social aplomb and apparent immunity to the law. The psychological dimensions of the crimes, more than any technical ones, are the strongest evidence, and criminal profiling is more like witchcraft than art, let alone science. Yet I can't think of any common criminal doing what the crook did, and when the impossible has been eliminated, the improbable must be true. Pederson isn't beholden to local elites as the Dallas police were, and with the suspect dead for decades, she can name names.

*The King of Diamonds* exposes many of the more freakish skeletons of Dallas in the 1960s, and the weird effects of big new money. Since the crime is burglary rather than murder, the book avoids the worst morbid excesses of true crime as a genre. It starts with theft and builds to a sociological study of a slice of America, like *The Wire* but with bigger hair and a lot more money. Conventional wisdom depicts the victims as the people who built Dallas, the pillars of society. Pederson shows them as vain, desperately trying to outdo each other in excess and owing their success more to luck and grasping greed than any kind of good judgement. Their bogeyman came from their own class.
