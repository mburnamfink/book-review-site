Books are weighty, important objects. They're sacred writ, durable expressions of ideas, what tyrants burn, and man's best friend outside a dog. But the bookshelf, well, that's just furniture, and simple furniture at that. I was not familiar with Petroski, and I am astounded at the history he uncovered, and equally astounded that a civil engineering professor writes with such humor and balance.

Medieval and Renaissance depictions of scholars at work in their studies show books on desks, in piles, and laid haphazardly on individual shelves. The modern bookshelf, with books stored vertically with spines out, did not become standardized until shockingly late, with clear evidence emerging for bookshelves only in the 17th century. Getting there was a long and awkward journey. In the ancient world, when writing was mostly in scrolls, scrolls were stored either in pigeonholes or in a box of related scrolls, each tagged for easy identification. As the scroll was replaced by the codex, pigeonholes were replaced by specialized bookchests and armoires. Richly decorated codices had to be protected from thieves and each other, as the metal studded covers would destroy adjacent materials.

Monasteries balanced the demands of security and access by developing the chained lectern. Books were laid out on angled reading tables, ideally perpendicular to windows to provide light. To keep the books from wandering off, they were chained to the furniture. A shelf above the reading surface allowed books not actively in use to be stored, but these were not modern bookshelves. Libraries were small enough (a major monastery library might have a few hundred books) that the librarians could distinguish books by their covers and page edges alone. Books were stored lying flat so the covers could be seen, or with the page edges facing out. The spine was a functional hinge, which properly should have been hidden away.

Libraries have had space concerns almost since they started, but when reading was only practical by daylight those space concerns mostly encompassed seats by windows. It was only with the invention of the printing press that books became commonplace enough to be safely unchained and need denser storage. It took decades if not centuries for the modern book to emerge. Originally, books were printed by publishers, sold as loose quires by booksellers, and then bound according to the taste and budget of the purchaser by bookbinders.

Samuel Pepys was one a forward-looking early modern reader. His books were handsomely bound, with the authors and titles on the spines to distinguish them. He also had the problem of more books than shelves, and regularly wrote of his intention to get rid of old books to make space for the new. But he, like most of us, got more shelves rather than finding ways to have fewer books. 

The bookshelf itself is a compromise between cost, strength, and appearance. Long shelves sag according to the fourth power of their length, while extra vertical supports steal space that could be used for books. Wood possesses warmth, while steel is stronger. Densely packed floor to ceiling shelves store the most books, but corridors have to be wide enough for readers to traverse, and low and high shelves are awkward to reach. Human factors sadly conflict with the goal of maximum books per cubic foot.

Once shelves are present, they need books, and there are many ways to arrange books. Petroski offers 26 organization schemes, from alphabetical to Dewey decimal to physical qualities like height to date of acquisition, and even esoteric arrangements known only to the organizer. On the shelf, books can either have their spines aligned at various depths, or be pushed all the way back to leave a ragged front to the shelf. And books remain inconveniently non-standard in size, with the exception of now-discontinued mass market paperbacks. While trade paperbacks and hardcovers vary by a few inches, non-standard books can differ greatly in size. Petroski uses an example of a pair of books on skyscrapers and bridges, which would be almost impossible to shelve next to one another. Ideally, a shelf would have the books standing against each other, without being so tightly compressed that they're impossible to move, but the realities of constantly expanding collections mean real bookshelves are either overpacked or require bookends to hold books up.

*The Book on the Bookshelf* is a delightful history of an under-appreciated object, with amusing anecdotes and practical advice for personal bookshelves. It opened my eye to a piece of furniture I've always overlooked, and how I can better arrange my own library.
