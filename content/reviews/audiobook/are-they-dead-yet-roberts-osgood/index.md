*Are They Dead Yet?* is a cute look at what could be a grim art form by one of its foremost practitioners. The individual stories are interesting, but whenever Roberts tries to generalize, the book becomes a dull literature review.

The book is at its best when Roberts keeps it personal. He first became aware of death as a young child, when the execution of Julius and Ethel Rosenberg was headline news. He describes himself as unusually afraid of death, in a Jewish family that didn't talk much about dying. He started in journalism at the anarchic *New York Daily News*, where obits were reserved for members of the newspaper family, including behind-the-scenes workers like printers and deliverymen, and then transitioned to the much more staid *New York Times*. He basically fell into obituaries late in his career, and found a surprisingly vibrant journalistic subfield.

The obituary as a form is full of paradoxes. It celebrates a person certain not to read it, barring some unlikely errors. Everybody dies, but only a few people are notable enough to warrant an obituary in a major paper. A death is often not really news, yet it's important to get these capsule bios out quickly, and so the *Times* has about 2,000 pre-written obituaries. Roberts has lots of stories about the remarkable people he's gotten to meet in his career. My favorites were an aerospace engineer who designed a solar shield for the Skylab mission, a Catholic priest who lived far past the six months his cancer doctors estimated and inspired this book, and a New Jersey woman who opted for assisted suicide on her own weeks-left cancer prognosis.

He cites a number of secondary works, to the point of redundancy. Of course obituaries reflect the values of the societies which produce them. Of course white men are over-represented compared to their demographics. In 1900, pastors were especially common in the obit pages; in the 2020s, it's actors. An obituary is the first draft of a biography, and often the only one. Finding what is noteworthy is more art than science.

This is a fine light read and works best when Roberts sticks to what he knows. It'd definitely be a fantastic 10k-word magazine article, but there's not enough content for a book. Like a beloved but unexceptional grandparent, it merits a fond paid notice, not the fully scoped deal.
