*The Everywhere Millionaire* is a cutting and incisive book on public policy, which overcomes the intellectual orthodoxy of its economics professor authors to deliver a key message about inequality and power in America. While tech billionaires dominate the headlines and the Forbes 400, most of the wealth of the top 1% is at a substantially lower level, the deca- and centi-millionaire who owns something like the second largest installer of aluminum siding in the tri-state area (to synthesize an imaginary subject of this book). These more mundane wealthy people live almost everywhere, effectively *are* American politics, and have changed the tax code to their immense advantage over the past 40 years.

The authors first got a hint of the problem working at the Treasury's Office of Tax Analysis in 2014, where they did a lot of data plumbing in the IRS's steam-powered antique of a database to link the tax returns of wealthy individuals to their businesses to their employees. This synoptic view of the American economy feels like a *Seeing Like a State 101* administrative mapping, but is apparently nearly impossible. The results are stunning. The Forbes 400 are collectively worth $4 trillion. The three million "everywhere millionaires" average around $25 million apiece, and together are worth *$54.8 trillion* as of 2025, when this book was written.

Much of the wealth in America is concentrated in the hands of privately held owner-operated businesses structured as S corporations, which pass through the business's profits to the owner's personal income without additional taxes. These pass-through companies account for half of the increase in income of the top 0.1%. The top 1% of business owners take home $570 billion annually. With substantially better data, Zidar and Zwick can be more precise than Piketty in *Capital in the 21st Century*, and point to exactly who is getting rich, and how.

While these companies are lauded as "small family businesses", they often feel quite large, with hundreds of employees and annual revenues in the $10-$100 million range. $10 million doesn't take a lot: a half-dozen fast-food franchises can hit that, if they're in good locations and well run. Some of these small family businesses are in the billion-dollar income range. And yet, being "small family businesses", they aren't subject to a host of regulations, from the Family and Medical Leave Act, which only applies to companies with more than 50 employees, to Title VII anti-discrimination rules at 15. Stay small if you want to avoid hiring *those people* and generously give new mothers 48 hours to recover from giving birth. It's still possible to be worth tens of millions of dollars without crossing into medium-sized business.

All of this public policy stuff is genuinely fascinating, but I nearly drowned as the book began glazing these entrepreneur owners. Dick Portillo of Portillo's Hot Dogs is a stereotypical example, a Boomer dude who went from a hot dog cart to a nationwide chain through obsessive dedication to the dog. Great, another grinder with zero hobbies, intellectual development, or aesthetic sense. At least give us freaks like the Brockways, who opened the book. They spent $60 million on the wedding of the century, renting a French palace so 26-year-old Madelaine Brockway could marry Jacob LaGrone with the whole wedding party in Louis XIV cosplay. Shortly after the dream wedding, Jacob would be indicted for shooting at Texas cops after a domestic dispute with Madelaine and her father. Jacob got off with probation. The Brockways have no taste and way too much money, but if they won't give us bread, at least they can be the circus.

There are some solid demographics buried in the middle section. Being born rich is a significant benefit in becoming an everywhere millionaire, but isn't sufficient. Generational transitions are tricky, and the second generation are often mediocre managers, stereotypically being disinterested in the details, over-emotional, or rigidly conservative in a changing business climate. Companies that do transfer to heirs take advantage of sophisticated strategies for avoiding taxes on the transfers. One event business had father and son as partners, and then when COVID hit, they used the temporary tanking in value to transfer the company entirely to the son at a lower cash basis, while taking a PPP loan, laying off a bunch of employees (hey, wait a second!), and refocusing the business on digital marketing. A $50 million company got moved like it was worth $5 million. Only an absolute sap pays estate taxes.

After glossing up hardworking entrepreneurs in boring sectors, and the private equity firms who help founders cash out and become leisured investors, the book returns to the more interesting policy issues. Running for office is expensive, and many government roles are either part-time, like mayor and state legislator (a friend back in Arizona had a state representative as their Uber driver), or pay peanuts compared to a private-sector equivalent, like Congress. Politics is a hobby for people with money and time, which equates to established owner-operators. Fund-raising is a lot easier when you have friends with money, as businessmen who golf in the right circles frequently do.

The authors explore how four collective monopolies, held by a cartel rather than individuals, drive up prices for all of us. The American Medical Association has lobbied for restricting America to 100,000 Medicare-funded residency slots per year since the early 90s, which contributes to fewer doctors than in other OECD countries and high medical costs. The AMA also blocks physician assistants and nurse practitioners from diagnostic and prescribing activities they are perfectly qualified to do. Car dealers and beer distributors are protected by franchise laws, and states are unwilling to touch these major categories of taxable sales. Realtors control access to real estate listings and maintain a cartel over commissions, increasing the price of housing by a few percent and creating perverse incentives to not get the best deal for buyers.

The final chapter, on how the tax code has been systematically bent to enable owner-operators of pass-throughs to avoid taxes, is among the most important parts of the book. Both of Trump's major tax bills let business owners deduct practically everything. The owner of an S corp doesn't have to fairly pay into Medicare because they can give themselves a nominal salary, say $150k, and take the other $850k in profits. Those profits aren't taxed much either, thanks to cuts in rates for the top income brackets. I don't recall Zidar and Zwick giving a specific number on how much the Treasury has lost, but hundreds of billions is plausible. Owner-operators have sucked up the lion's share of economic growth since 1990, with shareholders the rest, and workers mere crumbs.

At the beginning and end of the book, Zidar and Zwick remember that they are human beings and treat their subjects with the kind of disdain normally reserved for parasitic wasps. Unfortunately, in between they are economic professors and are impressed at how much surplus value these creatures are sucking up. In some ways, everyday millionaires are sympathetic; at least they're doing real jobs and taking risks with their own money, unlike investment bankers. But the absolute death grip they have on prosperity, and the constant whining about how beset they are by government regulations and ungrateful employees and idiotic customers, are the epitome of winning and mad about it.
