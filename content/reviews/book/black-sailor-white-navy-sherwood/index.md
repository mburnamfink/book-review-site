*Black Sailor, White Navy* is a close study of one of the darker moments in US naval history, the 1972 race riot on the *USS Kitty Hawk* and subsequent racial unrest throughout the fleet. Sherwood contextualizes detailed descriptions of the major incidents against the sociological context of the early 1970s and the reforms of Chief of Naval Operations Admiral Elmo Zumwalt Jr. The *Kitty Hawk* riot prompted Congressional hearings and a major re-evaluation of racial policy in the US Navy. Sherwood's book is a solid academic account of institutional racism, its sources are overwhelmingly officers' accounts, which gives the book a pro-Navy institutional bias.

The Army and Marines had experienced their own bouts of racial violence in the late 60s, with disorganized conflicts at the line level as well as a dramatic uprising at Long Binh Jail. The Navy had not yet experienced major unrest because for most of the Vietnam War it had served as a refuge for educated, motivated, and white volunteers who didn't want to be drafted as infantry for the war. In the early 70s, decreasing US combat commitments and the transformation to the all-volunteer force meant that the Navy began taking in more Black sailors.

These Black sailors arrived into a Navy that carried a heavy legacy of institutional racism. Black sailors had been largely restricted to steward ratings from the early 20th century to the start of World War II. After the armed services had been integrated, Blacks made up a lower percentage of sailors, and a much lower percentage of chief petty officers and commissioned officers. The Navy was a technical service and many positions required an A-school certification that Black enlistees from impoverished and segregated backgrounds struggled to obtain. Without a technical cert, sailors were limited to simpler jobs like scraping paint, moving ordnance, and mess and laundry duties. Being junior enlisted in the Navy at the time was objectively a thankless and painful job, and a chain of command which prioritized mission success could seem anywhere from indifferent to actively hostile.

As CNO, Zumwalt recognized these challenges. He had to transition to the all-volunteer force, replace obsolete World War II ships with new craft, prosecute the most aggressive phase of the Vietnam War with the Linebacker bombings, and keep within declining budgets. Zumwalt was well aware of the need for racial reforms, and had what Sherwood describes as a radical consciousness. Zumwalt's views were sharpened by his special assistant for minority affairs, Lieutenant Commander William Norman, an African American officer who had tendered a letter of resignation in frustration over the slow pace of reform during the prior decade. The center of Zumwalt's reforms was his 1970 message Z-66, which directed every ship and installation to appoint a minority affairs officer, and set up councils and training to bring minority issues to the attention of the chain of command.

All of this was a decent start, but the problems were deep-seated. The *Kitty Hawk* riot was the first and most dramatic example of racial unrest. *USS Kitty Hawk* had been deployed in support of Operation Linebacker for nearly nine months, with its tour extended because other carriers had mechanical problems. The crew was worn to a frazzle. Captain Marland Townsend was relatively new in command, and had made several disciplinary choices since June which had put Black members of the ship's company on edge. In several cases of minor scuffles between the crew, Black sailors had suffered severe punishments such as 30 days in the brig and potential court-martial, while white participants had gotten off with fines and warnings. Townsend defended his actions as in keeping with the prior disciplinary records of the participants, but the appearance of racial bias was hard to ignore. Ship policy did not help. Sailors were allowed to choose where they berthed, which meant that the ship was socially segregated. This was voluntary on both sides of the color line, but it amplified "us-vs-them" feelings. Captain's Mast disciplinary hearings were broadcast on closed-circuit TV. Captain Townsend intended this to be fair and educational about how the Navy worked, but many sailors treated the broadcasts as entertainment. Black sailors felt the broadcasts were a public humiliation.

After a liberty in Subic Bay, which was marked by several more incidents of drunken racial violence, including allegations that a white sailor paid a group of Filipinos to assault a Black sailor, the ship returned to Yankee Station. A minor scuffle in the mess hall caused a cook to call out the ship's Marine riot squad, and events escalated rapidly. There were a series of clashes by small groups throughout the ship between Black sailors, the Marines, and white sailors. A carrier is a warren of passages and compartments, and the officers had little visibility into what was going on. In a stroke of good fortune, one of the few Black officers in the Navy was the ship's executive officer, and Commander Benjamin Cloud managed to deescalate the violence by appealing to the participants as a Black man.

The *Kitty Hawk* incident, along with similar incidents on the carrier *USS Constellation* and oiler *USS Hassayampa*, prompted Congressional hearings on the racial disorder, nominally framed around "permissiveness" in the fleet, but in actuality a stalking horse for groups opposed to Zumwalt. Navy conservatives wanted Zumwalt's reforms overturned, and Dixiecrat racists wanted to make a case against integration in general. Louisiana Congressman F. Edward Hébert was the driving force behind the hearings. Perhaps my favorite moment in the book was when John Baker, a Black sailor involved in the *Constellation* protest, was called as a witness and immediately flipped the proceedings into an attack on poor conditions in the Navy. These powerful Congressmen were unprepared to handle a Black radical with personal knowledge of events and a political orientation that explained why they had occurred.

Sherwood is sympathetic to Zumwalt's efforts, but he evaluates approaches based around consciousness-expansion as failures. Participants in UPWARD (Understanding Personal Worth and Racial Dignity), an encounter-group-inspired racial awareness seminar, reported more racial animus *after* the trainings. Admiral James Holloway III, the CNO who followed Zumwalt, eliminated many of the more radical approaches, replacing them with affirmative action, a top-down ban on the use of racist slurs, and advertising campaigns in Black media to change minority opinion of the Navy and improve the quality of recruits. Sherwood argues that Holloway solidified what Zumwalt started and largely fixed the Navy. Like every other institution in America, the Navy has not solved its racial problem, but the number of minority chiefs and officers is closer to the population as a whole. The riots did not recur, though I suspect a slackening of operational tempo with the end of the Vietnam War has more explanatory power than any reforms.

*Black Sailor, White Navy* is a detailed study of some key incidents in American military history. Unfortunately, Sherwood is limited by his choice of sources. The book relies almost entirely on JAGMAN investigations of the riots, contemporary reports written by Navy lawyers acting as internal investigators. Sherwood is upfront that the Navy of the time was institutionally racist, but does not make the leap that the JAGMAN reports he relied on for this book could also be biased in the same way. From this book alone, the racial violence appears inexplicable and largely Black-originated, with groups of African-American sailors ambushing white sailors. The [Wikipedia article on the *Kitty Hawk* riot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Kitty_Hawk_riot) depicts the incidents as involving much more mutual "self-defense" by sailors of both races, with Black injuries somehow noting making it into the official record. Moreover, the *Kitty Hawk* Marines, rather than being a force for order, appear to have participated in a classic "police riot", using their authorization to deploy force arbitrarily and unevenly. Wikipedia draws this version of events from [*Against All Tides*](https://www.chicagoreviewpress.com/against-all-tides-products-9781641607841.php), a 2023 book by Marv Truhe. Truhe was a defense attorney for some of the *Kitty Hawk* crew, and his book is going on my stack.

While I think Sherwood could have treated his sources more skeptically, he provides a detailed account of many confusing incidents, contextualizes the riots in the broader events of the era and the unique culture of the Navy, and ultimately takes institutional racism seriously.
