*The Rock Laid Bare* sticks the landing.

Oh, you want to know how it sticks the landing? *A Haunt for Jackals* closed with a bomb of personal revelation. *The Rock Laid Bare* plays out the mortal consequences of the new situation, burning through every last reserve the protagonists have as they ride rebellion towards triumph or destruction. Failure means a shameful death, and even victory *hurts*.

The politics are fraught but take a backseat to even stronger character dynamics. The characters are driven relentlessly by their passions in the face of obliteration. Azetla is torn between his righteousness, contempt for his father, and love for Tzal. For her part, Tzal is deadly competent and viciously nihilistic, absolutely sure love is the last and worst of her curses. The secondary cast—Azetla's parents, friends left behind to face the bloodthirsty Emperor Riada—have their own issues around doubt, fear, and loyalty. There are confessions, confrontations, oaths, accusations, battles that will echo through history, and the bloody toll of victory. Where *By Blood, By Salt* was languid, *Rock* hammers its emotional blows every chapter and pulls no punches at the conclusion.

Odom has a fine awareness of her characters' psychology. Two scenes stand above a mountain range of excellent writing like stratovolcanos. The opening flashback sees a teenage Azetla betrayed by his father King Keved to the blood sacrifices of the empire, the ultimate coward's choice to trade away a son's life for his own position. The second great scene has Tzal confronting King Keved at the grand assembly of his people and utterly destroying him without laying a single finger on the royal body.

Fantasy novels are defined by their imagined histories, and Odom keeps hers grounded. There is no magic, no giant eagles coming to save anyone. The Maruwan Empire feels like Rome, as the archetypal empire, but also has Aztec blood sacrifices and English names. The tribes of the south feel very Pashtun, but they are *tribes* plural, and almost as different from each other as they are from the settled people to the north. I do wish there was more creativity in the setting, but Odom blends her palette of historical inspirations well, and avoids all of the major sins of cheap world-building. The setting exists to serve the characters and their stories, and more muted colors are appropriate for the background.

Against that muted background, religion is where the series shows its color. Odom treats faith with real seriousness, including a realistic ambiguity around the divine: our main characters were born with signs and portents, but their destinies are very much made and not foretold. Each religion has its own subtleties. The southern tribes worship the same desert god as the Mashevi, but the practice and expression of their faith are very different. Maruwan is centered around bloody human sacrifice, which serves to unite believers and purge them of doubts, not merely show that madmen wield the knifen of empire. Mashevism, the primary religion, gets the deepest treatment, and it is based on Judaism. Azetla started as Jewish-coded, with his food restrictions, fasts, and despised-minority ethnicity. His Mashevism grows in importance and complexity through the series, becoming a religion of exiles, fanatics, ignorant peasants, and acculturated aristocrats, with many similarities to the Second Temple period. I'm very sensitive to the misuse and abuse of Jewish tropes, and Odom handles her stand-in religion with care. These are a people who wrestle with God.

In much the same way, this whole series wrestles with the idea of mission. Both Azetla and Tzal are driven by deeply held beliefs about who they are, restorer and destroyer. They pull the other characters with them, like flotsam caught in a powerful current, and are themselves more complex than the mission that has honed them to a razor's edge. *Land of Exile* does character-driven fantasy right.
