Hugh "Stuffy" Dowding is one of the unsung heroes of the Second World War. In an era when professional opinion was "the bomber always gets through", Dowding had the vision and drive to imagine an integrated air defense and then build it, binding radar and fighters together with a state-of-the-art communications system and the command-and-control technology of the big map room. Dowding was cruelly removed from command shortly after winning the Battle of Britain and faded into relative obscurity until his contributions were restored by more recent histories.

Fisher's approach is best exemplified by a line in the book where he says flat-out, "In this tale, Dowding is my hero. Leigh-Mallory (mutinous No. 12 Group commander) and Bader (legless squadron commander) are my villains." An objective history is impossible, but I think authors should be a little more attentive to balance. The central Battle of Britain narrative is decent, if unremarkable, but this biased attitude colors the book.

The strength of a tale-first approach is storytelling. Fisher has an eye for the ridiculous in the war. An early test of the radar system, with King George in attendance, became a fiasco because the first radar models couldn't distinguish targets in front of the array from targets behind. A single rogue French liaison plane triggered a cascade of squadrons intercepting bogeys that were squadrons that had just taken off on their own intercepts. Robert Watson-Watt took credit for the invention of radar, while his assistant Arnold Wilkins did the engineering work. Churchill's science advisor Professor Lindemann shows up as a buffoon obsessed with aerial mines, who is so obstreperous that committees are dissolved and reformed without him so that work can get done.

Against all of these characters, Dowding is the sole sane, reasonable man. Reserved and fundamentally unsuited to the clubhouse officer culture of the Edwardian British Army, Dowding earned the nickname Stuffy. As a junior officer in colonial India, he saw the potential for aerial reconnaissance and counter-insurgency well before others. During the First World War, he led a combat squadron, and then was kicked upstairs to a training command after a spat with his commander. Between the wars, he served in staff research posts, overseeing the tactical development of air defense, and its related infrastructure of radar and hard-surfaced airfields. Dowding rose slowly, with his competence and stubborn defense of his positions balancing out an utter inability to play interservice political games. He was the right man for the era, and likely saved Britain by holding back fighter squadrons during the fall of France, but his lack of allies and opposition to the "big wing" tactics of Leigh-Mallory and Bader proved his downfall. Leigh-Mallory and Bader wanted to confront the air attack on an even basis and win decisively; Dowding correctly saw that his objective was to maintain the Royal Air Force as a fleet-in-being and prevent invasion while attriting the Luftwaffe.

But Stuffy wasn't entirely a stuffed shirt, and as the stress of battle wore on, he began to receive messages from beyond the grave. He told others of his visions involving dead fighter pilots and his deceased wife as a psychopomp during the battle, and wrote several fringe books on the subject after his retirement, which were politely ignored by the rest of the world. Some American pilots once mistakenly assumed that the dotty old Lord Dowding must be the father of the efficient military commander Air Chief Marshal Dowding. Spiritualism was a fad during the period, and Dowding was far from alone, if a little past the heyday of the movement. Sherlock Holmes creator Arthur Conan Doyle was obsessed with fairies, and physicist William Crookes firmly believed in psychic powers and ghosts. Writing even-handedly about a belief system which has aged as badly as spiritualism is difficult, and Fisher cannot help but mock Dowding's occultism.

What sold me on this book was the blurb's claim that Dowding had an intense mystical and occult bent. Lots of people think God is on their side in a war. Dowding was more literal than most in believing that angels were telling him to trust in victory. But the occult angle is poorly grafted onto a generic lightweight pop military history. Even if spiritualism is ridiculous, there would be genuinely valuable work in summarizing the beliefs Dowding laid out in his long-out-of-print small-press books, and that doesn't occur here. This book is fast and readable, and doesn't make any major factual mistakes, but it's also thinly written. Pendle's [*Strange Angel: The Otherworldly Life of Rocket Scientist John Whiteside Parsons*](/book/strange-angel-the-otherworldly-life-of-rocket-scientist-john-whiteside-parsons-a-portrait-of-the-jpl-founder-genius-rocketry-and-the-occult-pendle) showed you can write about the history of science and occultism seriously, and Korda's [*With Wings Like Eagles: A History of the Battle of Britain*](/book/with-wings-like-eagles-a-history-of-the-battle-of-britain-korda) is the best Battle of Britain book I've read.
