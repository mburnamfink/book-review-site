Edgerton takes a strongly antagonistic view towards techno-boosterism, and a general whiggish history. In his view, innovation cannot be meaningfully encouraged and directed, technological improvement is not a fundamental force in the 20th century, and technology itself is not a useful category. This book is more polemic than academic, and the best point it makes, that the past is always with us, is not a proper answer to the arguement that technology creates a different future. Boosters see change as good, critics like Edgerton see change as bad, and in both cases this is primarily an emotional reaction rather than a considered choice about the values we hold and the lives we want to live.

The most interesting stories Edgerton tells are about how older technologies persist. Horsepower, the use of animals for labor, peaked around 1940, which is far later than most people expect. Steam locomotives persisted through the 1970s, and thermal power plants are *steam* power plants. High technology is frequently uneconomic. Nuclear reactors, supersonic aircraft, rocketry, and major pharmaceutical projects rarely pay off in economic terms. R&D funding has not led to economic growth, and the fastest-growing economies, like the USSR in the 1930s or Asian countries in the 1980s, invest relatively little in research. Even over the long run, R&D appears to have little correlation with development. In 1900, Britain was vastly more wealthy than Italy, and by 2000 the two countries converged in terms of GDP per capita despite Britain's investments in radar, jet aircraft, and microelectronics, and Italy's laissez-faire approach to high-level scientific investments.

Edgerton begins the book by saying that focusing on things rather than technologies is a better lens: who owns things, who controls things, where do things come from? Technologies are not physical objects; they are ways of categorizing physical objects. [*The Nature of Technology: What It Is and How It Evolves*](/book/the-nature-of-technology-what-it-is-and-how-it-evolves-arthur) is roughly contemporary with *The Shock of the Old*, and has a much stronger and more usable theory of technology based around modular encapsulation of physical principles harnessed to human ends.

All of Edgerton's examples are true, and yet this book doesn't convince me. The argument, trivially extended, says that there is little substantially different between how we live now and how we lived 100 years ago, or life in the United States and life in sub-Saharan Africa. The difference is the *things* surrounding the different lifestyles, and the novel materials, forms, and control systems embedded in the modern version, as well as the ease of their manufacturing. The old persists because it is difficult to fully replace a working system, and yet the new spreads like tendrils of ink, until we can barely see the substrate of the old.

It's rare that an academic critique manages to be relevant decades after publication, but *The Shock of the Old* would feel outdated even when it came out. Its history seems to end somewhere around 1995, with a few years of post-Soviet collapse, industrializing China, and skepticism towards this wacky idea of letting ordinary people use networked computers. 2007 saw the release of both this book and the iPhone. Smartphones are a technology at least as revolutionary as the car, and one which has diffused far faster.

I've read a lot of STS critiques of technology, and Edgerton is distinctly behind Langdon Winner, Bruno Latour, and Lewis Mumford as a serious thinker about technology. *The Shock of the Old* pushes hard against techno-progressive arguments, but misses the difference between 'better' and 'different' as it offers up the long legacy of actually existing technology.
