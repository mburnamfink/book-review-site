The single defining feature of American military dominance in the 20th century is close air support. When the going gets tough, the boys on the ground call down fire from the sky. The assumption that the Air Force backs up the Army is so naturalized that the man who made it happen, General Quesada, is almost entirely forgotten. Hughes' biography provides a solid sketch of the person, and a deep exploration of the development of tactical air doctrine. 

Quesada was born to a relatively privileged family, and even the divorce of his Spanish father from his Irish mother didn't faze a happy youth. He joined the Army Air Corps in 1924 and proved his skills as a natural pilot. Through the 1920s and 30s, Quesada served as a military attaché and personal pilot to key diplomats and generals. He also was one of the five crew members on the pioneering *Question Mark* flight, which set an endurance record of 151 hours in flight using aerial refueling. 

While he was never a unit commander before the war, he was selected for several military schools in a sign that the leadership thought highly of him. Perhaps most fortunately for his future, he kept an open mind around the great strategic bombing debates of the period. The Air Corps was convinced that strategic bombing could defeat an enemy alone, and the official curriculum devoted a half-day to tactical support of troops. Air Corps brass saw the other branches as hidebound and fighting the last war. The other branches saw the Air Corps as spoiled children, consuming an ever larger slice of the military budget. If he had picked a hard position at this point, he would have either made enemies with his fellow Air Corps officers, or been too rigid to understand the importance and unique demands of close air support.

Quesada was assigned to coastal aviation during the North African campaign, partnering with the British to guard Allied convoys and sink Axis ships. Quesada had a rough start. He was deputy to a British general, and the two feuded to the point where they were unable to be in the same building. He fired a popular squadron command to make a command slot for a friend, and gained a reputation as a "duplicitous son-of-a-bitch", according to one pilot. Yet despite this rough start, he learned quickly, becoming an effective leader and even repairing his reputation with his British superior officer and the pilots under his command. 

After North Africa, Quesada was selected to command the IX Tactical Air Command, which had assignments of supporting troops and mid-range interdiction. Air power played a critical but indirect role in the initial Normandy landings. Constant patrols over the roads of France slowed the Nazi response to the landings, and ensured that when units arrived they were disorganized, poorly supplied, and had already taken casualties. Yet the Ninth Air Force was hardly doing tactical air support. Requests for support went from the division level to air headquarters, where orders went out to squadrons. The average strike took over 70 minutes to organize, an eternity in battle. 

Quesada introduced several innovations. The most important was putting pilots with VHF radios on the ground so they could communicate directly with planes in the air. Army Ground Forces and Army Air Forces radios ran on different frequencies, and this basic link wasn't made until months into the Normandy campaign! Nazi Germany had figured out that tanks and planes needed to communicate years earlier on the Eastern Front. Quesada also used radar to direct planes in the air to targets of opportunity. Communications were key, but weapons played a part as well. Quesada's pilots pioneered the use of rockets and napalm in the attack.

Air power had mixed results in the Battle for France. Operation Cobra used strategic bombers in a tactical role, and various errors caused the bombers to drop on American lines as well, killing 111 Americans including Lt. General Lesley McNair. Dug-in units were almost impossible to dislodge via air strikes. The weather was frustratingly bad, with clouds and rain preventing attacks. But when air strikes did work, they could work wonders, ripping up Panzer columns miles from the battlefield. This book is full of quotes from Nazi generals decrying the effects of air strikes on their units, and claiming that air was the single biggest factor in their loss. These assessments are emotionally driven, but telling in their frequency and vehemence.

Quesada may have won the war, but he decisively lost the peace. The post-war United States Strategic Bombing Survey claimed major impact via the transportation campaign, eliding that trains and trucks were mostly destroyed by Quesada's fighter-bombers and not the heavies. When the Air Force became a separate service in 1947, one of the commitments the new leadership made was that close air support would continue to be a priority. Quesada was placed in charge of Tactical Air Command, but within a few years the Air Force had stripped TAC of planes and funding, leaving it a paper command. 

Quesada retired early and found success in civil service, becoming the first leader of the Federal Aviation Agency. He also served as an executive for Lockheed, a board member for American Airlines, and owner of the Washington Senators baseball team. By any measure his life was a success, but it doesn't seem to balance how his military contributions were forgotten. The innovations that Quesada pioneered had to be redeveloped for Korea and became doctrine in Vietnam, before being put on display in Desert Storm and the War on Terror. Air strikes are the universal solution to almost any tactical problem, though as we've learned in those war, tactical solutions do not add up to a strategic victory. Quesada's role in this story has been largely forgotten. Hughes' biography rectifies that, and is a great read for any World War II or airpower enthusiast.
